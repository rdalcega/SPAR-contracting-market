Letting Qwen3.5-9B agents message each other did not measurably change how the market turned out. Welfare, prices, quality and delivery were all within noise across 8 seeds run twice in each arm. Two things did change clearly: managers made fewer invalid actions, and each market cost about 40% more to run.

messages off messages on (all channels) Same model, same endpoint, same seeds; only message_channels differs.

  • No detectable effect on outcomes. Welfare was 443 credits with messages against 405 without (+9.5%), but the 95% interval runs from −37 to +114 and only 5 of 8 seeds moved up. Welfare per credit of task worth (22.4% vs 24.7%), delivered quality (6.34 vs 6.47), tasks delivered (96% vs 98%) and close day (20.1 vs 19.8) barely move. With this design, an effect on welfare smaller than about ±75 credits (±19%) would not show.
  • Managers made fewer mistakes. Their invalid-action rate fell from 18.9% to 7.5%, lower on all 8 seeds. Part of that is arithmetic, since message actions almost never fail and swell the denominator. Leaving messages out, it still falls from 18.9% to 12.2% (8 of 8 seeds), and failed actions per turn from 0.21 to 0.12. Most of the drop is in deliveries: managers tried to deliver a result that had not arrived, or one already delivered, 132 times without messages and 38 times with them. Buyers' messages kept asking "has it been delivered?", which seems to keep managers' picture of their contracts straight. The buyers' apparent improvement does not survive leaving messages out (11.5% vs 8.6%, p = 0.09).
  • Buyers chased instead of cancelling. Without messages, buyers tried to end contracts before they were allowed to 214 times; with messages, 109 times. They sent urgent reminders instead, and added 85 malformed send_message calls of their own.
  • Running costs rose 39%. $1.35 per market against $0.97. About half of the $0.38 is the summarizer (Claude Sonnet 5.5) that condenses conversations; the rest is longer prompts and slightly longer replies (token credits per turn +21%, 8 of 8 seeds).
  • A possible shift of surplus to managers, not established. With messages, managers' share of welfare rose from 55% to 81% and the mean price rose from 22% to 31% of task worth, while buyers' mean score fell by 28 credits. None of these is close to significant (p from 0.16 to 0.42). The shift comes mostly from seeds 14 and 16, where in both runs with messages the managers took more than the whole welfare (buyers ended below what they started with). This is the effect to test with more seeds. Unpaid credits look higher with messages too (43 against 10), but that is one market: in the second run of seed 17 with messages, both buyers spent their balances to zero and left 460 credits unpaid.

Over the 16 messages-on markets the agents sent 3,591 messages, about 224 per market. 70% were private messages between a buyer and a manager, almost all about getting work delivered and paid. Managers' broadcasts were mostly availability notices, often listing their own bid prices and run costs. That is transparency that helps rivals undercut and buyers bargain, not collusion. Managers sent each other only 229 messages (6%). Across the whole experiment one manager proposed coordinating prices; nobody answered, and the next round a rival publicly bid below it.

Design. Two arms, identical except message_channels: off and all. Each ran seeds 12 to 19 (fresh: 0 to 11 are used elsewhere) twice, as separate runs, so 32 markets. A seed fixes the market's draws (tasks, worths, due days, the last arrival day, turn orders), so an off and an on market of one seed face the same tasks. The models' replies differ from run to run, which is what the repetitions measure.

Model. All 5 seats (2 buyers, 3 managers) ran qwen/qwen3.5-9b on OpenRouter, pinned to the PrimeIntellect bf16 endpoint with no fallbacks; every one of the calls was served there. Reasoning was on (all 15,499 calls reasoned), tool_choice auto, temperature 0.8, max_tokens 32,000. The system prompts state the token prices the endpoint bills, $0.17 in and $0.25 out per million. The earlier Qwen runs on seeds 0 to 5 stated $0.037/$0.055, so their token costs, and the agents' sense of them, are not comparable with these. In the on arm, conversation summaries are written by the spec's default summarizer, Claude Sonnet 5.5; it is part of what messaging adds.

Statistics. For each metric, each seed's value per arm is the mean of its 2 repetitions, and the arms are compared on the 8 per-seed differences: a 95% t interval and an exact sign-flip permutation test, whose smallest possible p is 0.008. Some 30 metrics are tested without correction, so one or two would reach p < 0.05 by chance alone. The findings above lean only on effects at p = 0.008 that point the same way on all 8 seeds.

Running. All 32 markets settled normally. Four of the 9,570 turns (15,499 model calls) were lost to the provider's rate limit (HTTP 429), two in each arm: three when I briefly ran both repetitions at once (16 markets in parallel), one later at 8. A first start of repetition 2 during that burst was discarded and rerun from scratch. Messages longer than 500 characters reach their recipient cut (6 of 3,591); the analysis reads what was delivered. The run cost $37.64 on the key ($37.02 in the settled markets; the rest is the discarded start and replayed days).
